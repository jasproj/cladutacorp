Green Coffee Bean Storage and Shipping: Buyer's Guide

Updated June 20, 2026
March 2026  ·  5 min read  ·  By Claduta Corporation

Green coffee is a living agricultural product. Unlike roasted coffee, it continues to age and change after harvest — moisture migration, off-gas release, and microbial activity all affect quality in transit and storage. Getting the handling parameters right from warehouse to destination isn't optional: a shipment arriving at incorrect moisture or with mold damage can mean rejection, reprocessing costs, or lost customer relationships.

This guide covers the critical parameters for green coffee storage and shipping — the specifications that every buyer should confirm before and after shipment.

Why Green Coffee Handling Matters

Green coffee beans contain residual moisture from processing and are hygroscopic — they absorb or release moisture in response to ambient humidity. During a 20–35 day ocean voyage, coffee passing through tropical zones (particularly crossing the equator) is exposed to temperature swings and high humidity environments inside a container. Without correct packaging and moisture management, two failure modes occur:

  • Mold growth: At moisture above 12.5–13%, combined with high ambient humidity, mold (particularly Aspergillus species) can colonize beans during transit. Moldy coffee is unusable and may trigger mycotoxin (ochratoxin A) concerns that fail food safety screening.
  • Over-drying and brittleness: Coffee at below 10% moisture becomes brittle, fractures during roasting, and produces a flat, lifeless cup. Grinding also produces excessive fines at low moisture.

Moisture Specifications

The target moisture range for green coffee in international trade is 11.0–12.5%. The ICO (International Coffee Organization) recommends a maximum of 12.5% for shipments. Brazilian exporters targeting premium-market buyers typically ship at 11.5% as a target — with SGS-verified moisture at loading between 11.0–12.0%.

Water activity matters more than moisture: At any given moisture percentage, water activity (Aw) determines actual mold risk. Specify Aw <0.70 in your contracts — this is the threshold below which most mold species cannot grow, regardless of total moisture content.

Jute Bags vs GrainPro Inner Liners

Green coffee is typically packed in 60kg natural fiber (jute or sisal) bags. Jute is breathable — it allows the coffee to off-gas CO₂ naturally and equilibrate with ambient humidity. This has been the standard for over a century and works well for shorter transits or well-managed storage environments.

However, for longer transits (35+ days) or routes through tropical high-humidity zones, GrainPro inner liners — hermetically sealed poly bags placed inside the jute bag — provide significantly better protection against moisture ingress and contamination. GrainPro bags also extend shelf life by limiting oxidation, making them the recommended choice for specialty and high-value green coffee.

Container Specifications

Green coffee ships in standard dry containers. Key container requirements:

  • Container type: 20-foot dry container (most common) or 40-foot dry
  • Clean and dry: Containers must be inspected for cleanliness, odor-free (previous cargo odors can transfer), and free of moisture or pest contamination
  • Capacity: 20-foot container: 250–275 bags x 60kg = 15,000–16,500kg net. 40-foot container: up to 320 bags
  • Loading method: Bags are typically stacked on wooden pallets or direct floor, with dunnage (kraft paper, desiccant packets) placed to absorb moisture
  • Desiccants: Container desiccant poles (e.g., Dri-Box or equivalent) are placed along container walls to absorb humidity during transit — especially important for tropical routes

Temperature and Humidity Controls

Standard dry containers are not temperature-controlled. For most Brazilian origin shipments to destinations within the 20–35 day transit range, standard dry containers are appropriate. However, buyers routing through extremely hot transshipment ports or experiencing extended transit times due to routing disruptions should consider:

  • Requesting enhanced desiccant loading (more desiccant poles than standard)
  • Specifying GrainPro inner bags (eliminating ambient humidity as a risk factor)
  • Avoiding shipments during the hottest months for their route (relevant mainly for Middle East routes June–September)

Refrigerated containers (reefers) are used for extremely high-value or specialty green coffees by some buyers, but this is not standard practice in commodity-grade Brazilian Arabica or Conilon trade.

Port Storage Before Shipment

Green coffee may be held in port warehouses for days to weeks before vessel departure. At Port Santos and Port Vitória, coffee is stored in covered warehouses. Buyers should confirm with their exporter that warehouse conditions meet minimum standards: covered, dry, off the floor on pallets or dunnage, and free from chemical storage in the same facility (odor contamination risk).

SGS inspection at loading captures the moisture condition of the coffee at the moment of stuffing — not weeks earlier at the mill. If port storage exceeds 4 weeks, request a re-inspection at stuffing date.

What to Check on Arrival

When your shipment arrives, before accepting delivery:

  • Inspect container door seal for integrity — broken seals indicate possible tampering or moisture ingress
  • Check for visible condensation or moisture staining inside the container
  • Draw representative samples and test moisture immediately — compare to the SGS loading certificate
  • Check for off-odors (musty, sour, or chemical) indicating mold or contamination
  • Verify bag count and condition (torn bags, water damage marks)
  • Test a cupping sample against your contracted spec before accepting

Any variance from the contracted moisture specification discovered at destination should be documented immediately with photos and an independent inspector report, which forms the basis for a quality claim.
